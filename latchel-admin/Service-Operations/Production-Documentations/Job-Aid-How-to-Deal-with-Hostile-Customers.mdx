## ℹ️ Introduction

At Latchel we are committed to being customer-obsessed. We want every user to have the best possible experience using our services and interacting with our employees. Nevertheless, we are not willing to sacrifice our employees' well-being to achieve that.

### What is a Hostile Customer?

A hostile customer may be argumentative, use personal insults or inappropriate comments, yell or threaten associates.

At Latchel, we consider the following to be traits of a hostile customer:

- The customer is cursing or using inappropriate language
- The customer is making personal insults against the representative
- The customer is yelling or screaming and refuses to lower their voice
- The customer is making physical or other extreme threats
- The customer is making sexual advances or insinuations towards the associate

It's important to remember that not every customer who uses a loud voice or is persistent is necessarily aggressive. If you are able to calm them down using the techniques outlined below, you should proceed with the call as normal.

### De-escalation Techniques for Hostile Customers

Dealing with a hostile customer can be particularly challenging. Nobody should be expected to put up with angry customers being openly rude to them. However, there are some de-escalation techniques you should implement to try to calm the customer down. Find some tips below.

**Remain Calm**

Remember, hostile messages or calls aren't personal. It can be frustrating, but the customer is directing their anger at the situation—not at you specifically.

**Pick Your Words Wisely**

Words have a powerful impact on people's perception of reality. Therefore:

- Practice empathy
  - Try responding with phrases like, "I'm sorry you feel this way"; "May I suggest …"; "I'm so sorry to hear about this"; or "I will take action on this for you right away."
- Use positive language
  - Try to avoid negative terms like never, don't, and no. Instead, when dealing with an angry phone call, it's best to use positive language such as "yes", "definitely", "understand", and "recommend". By setting a positive tone, you are guiding the conversation.
- Consider your tone
  - Using an overly gentle tone may come across as patronizing, while a tone that's too firm can seem indifferent. Both extremes can escalate the situation. Aim for a steady, calm tone that conveys control, and a soft touch to show empathy and understanding.

**Let the Customer Vent**

Often, all a customer wants to do is get things off their chest. Let frustrated customers vent. That doesn't mean letting them use foul language or attack you personally. But recognize that anger and frustration come from not having a need met. If you listen carefully, you may be able to uncover the root cause of their issue.

In SMS, that means giving them space to express their concerns without immediately pushing back. This doesn't mean tolerating abusive language—personal attacks or foul language should still be addressed appropriately.

Once they have calmed down, you can ask them questions that will help get to the root cause of the issue.

**Set the Right Expectations**

Part of handling customer interactions is clearly explaining what's going on. Put yourself in their shoes—if you reached out about an issue, you'd want to understand the situation and know what to expect next. Setting realistic expectations helps build trust and prevents frustration if things don't go exactly as hoped.

**Adhere to a Script**

When handling a hostile customer, it might just be easier to follow a pre-written script to de-escalate things. This way you can make sure you say the right thing and don't get caught up in the heat of the moment. You'll find some examples below:

### Ending the conversation with a Hostile Customer

If your attempts to de-escalate a hostile customer are unsuccessful—whether over the phone or via SMS—you may end the conversation.

Before doing so, follow the steps outlined below to give the customer a chance to correct their behavior. This ensures we remain professional and give the customer a fair opportunity to re-engage respectfully.

## 📋 Step-by-Step Process

### STEP 1: Offer the customer a polite warning:

*Customer First Warning Call Script*

"I understand that this is frustrating; however, let's try to keep a professional tone so I can assist you with your concern."

*Customer First Warning SMS Script*

"I understand this is frustrating. I'm here to help, but I kindly ask that we keep the conversation respectful so I can assist you further."

*Customer First Warning Call/SMS Script*

"I understand your concern, but we don't tolerate the kind of language you are using."

### STEP 2: If the customer continues to curse or otherwise behave offensively, you will have to end the call/conversation:

*Customer Second Warning Call Script*

"[Customer name], I'm asking one more time to moderate your language/tone so I can further assist you. Otherwise, I will need to disconnect the phone call."

*Customer Second Warning SMS Script*

"[Customer Name], I'm asking one more time to keep the conversation respectful so I can continue helping you. If the language/tone doesn't change, I may need to end this conversation."

### STEP 3: Inform the customer that you are ending the conversation, and then end the call/SMS and stop responding.

*Customer Final Warning Call/SMS Script*

"I'm sorry, due to the repeated offensive language, I will now be ending this call/conversation. Please feel free to reach out again when you're ready to discuss the matter calmly. We'll be happy to assist you then."

### STEP 5: Take a step back.

These interactions—whether over the phone or via SMS—can be intense and emotionally draining. It's okay to take a few minutes to step away, breathe, get some water, or walk around to reset. Remember, it's not personal.

We also encourage you to bring up the experience with your Supervisor during your next 1:1 so they can offer support or guidance if needed.

Thank you for reporting the issue!

## 🎯 When to Break This SOP

Do not break or deviate from this SOP on your own authority. Always escalate to an Ops Support specialist or follow explicit instructions if you believe an exception is necessary.
